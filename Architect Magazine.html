Sheila Hicks Has Never Met an Object She Couldn’t Learn From

From Japanese staircases and African looms to miniature irons and humble ceramics, an intimate exhibition at Demisch Danant reveals the objects that have captured the pioneering fiber artist's imagination—and the lifelong curiosity that has taken her from corporate interiors to the world's leading museums.

What does an artist collect when she has spent a lifetime transforming ordinary materials into extraordinary works of art? For Sheila Hicks, the answer might be almost anything: a Japanese wooden staircase, an African weaving loom, a Chinese barber stool, a pair of snowshoes, or a handful of miniature irons. What connects these disparate objects is not their provenance or rarity, but the intelligence of the hands that made them and the unexpected possibilities they hold.

At Demisch Danant in New York, Sheila Hicks | Favorite Odds and Ends, on view through December 19, offers an unusually personal encounter with one of the most influential artists working in fiber today. Rather than presenting a conventional retrospective, the exhibition brings together objects Hicks has accumulated over decades of travel with examples of her own work, including woven minimes, sculptural boules, prayer rugs, and a monumental circular linen composition from the late 1970s.

The result is part exhibition, part cabinet of curiosities, and part autobiography told through things. It also raises a provocative question: Where does collecting end and artistic creation begin?

For Hicks, who has spent more than six decades challenging the distinctions between fine art, design, craft, and architecture, those boundaries have never been particularly convincing. Her work has consistently demonstrated that weaving can be monumental, that thread can possess the expressive force of sculpture, and that everyday techniques can become vehicles for radical experimentation.

Her collection offers a glimpse into the visual world that surrounds this practice, where tools, vessels, furniture, and unfamiliar artifacts coexist with her own compositions.

As Hicks puts it in a brief statement accompanying the exhibition:

Sneak into a quiet Space.

It might stay with you.

The invitation is characteristically elliptical, suggesting that an encounter with an object, however modest, can linger in the imagination long after the moment has passed.

A Lifetime of Looking

Born in Hastings, Nebraska, in 1934, Hicks studied at Yale University under Josef Albers, whose investigations into color and perception helped shape her approach to materials. Her subsequent travels through Latin America, particularly her engagement with indigenous weaving traditions, expanded her understanding of textiles as both a cultural language and an artistic medium.

Few artists have moved as effortlessly between fine art, architecture, and commercial design. Hicks, the subject of a major retrospective at the Centre Pompidou in Paris in 2018, has also collaborated with textile and furnishings companies including Knoll, Momentum Textiles, and Maharam. Indeed, long before her work received widespread institutional recognition, she was creating ambitious textile installations for some of the most prominent corporate interiors of the postwar era.

Among them were commissions for the Eero Saarinen-designed CBS headquarters in New York in 1966; the Ford Foundation headquarters, designed by Kevin Roche and John Dinkeloo, where she created monumental honey-colored bas-relief tapestries in 1967; and IBM’s Paris headquarters, for which she produced La Mémoire, a large-scale textile work, in 1972. For AT&T’s corporate campus in New Jersey, she created The Silk Rainforest (c. 1975), two monumental silk, linen, and cotton tapestries installed on either side of a glass staircase leading to the executive suites.

These projects were more than decorative commissions. At a time when corporate modernism favored glass, steel, and hard-edged geometries, Hicks introduced softness, texture, color, and a distinctly human presence into architectural interiors. Her work demonstrated that textiles could operate at the scale of architecture, transforming the experience of space rather than merely embellishing it.

Long based in Paris, Hicks has continued to explore the physical properties of fibers: their density, elasticity, color, texture, and capacity to be twisted, knotted, bundled, or suspended. Her small woven compositions, known as minimes, can be held in the hand, while her large-scale installations occupy walls, floors, and entire rooms.

The exhibition, part of Demisch Danant’s Studio Visits | Creative Spaces and Collected Lives series, brings this material sensibility into focus through a series of unexpected juxtapositions.

When a Staircase Becomes a Sculpture

Among the exhibition’s most striking works are two assemblages created in 2026 that incorporate Japanese wooden staircases.

In Ensemble of Japanese Staircase and Textile Panel, Hicks combines a staircase with a wool textile mounted on a panel. Measuring nearly seven feet tall, the composition brings together two different forms of construction: the rigid geometry of woodworking and the flexible structure of weaving.

The staircase, originally conceived as an instrument of movement and domestic utility, assumes an entirely different presence. Its steps and supports become a framework for considering rhythm, repetition, and spatial organization.

A companion work, Ensemble of Japanese Staircase and Ceramics with Ponytail, introduces ceramics and natural fibers into another wooden structure. The unexpected combination of hard and soft, geometric and organic, gives the assemblage an almost anthropomorphic character.

Both works demonstrate Hicks’ ability to recognize sculptural possibilities in objects that were never intended as sculpture.

Their inclusion also complicates the relationship between collecting and authorship. The staircases were made by other hands, for purposes unrelated to contemporary art. Hicks’ intervention lies in recognizing their potential and creating new relationships among disparate materials.

Elsewhere, an antique Chinese wooden barber stool supports one of Hicks’ signature boules, the compact, spherical fiber forms that have become recurring elements of her vocabulary. The ensemble turns a humble piece of furniture into a stage for an intimate sculptural encounter.

Another composition, Basket and Boule, similarly pairs a collected container with a contemporary fiber object. In Basket, Boules and Spindles, a woven basket holds an arrangement of linen, cotton, and wood, bringing together the tools, materials, and products of textile making.

These works suggest an artist who sees objects not as fixed entities but as participants in an ongoing process of transformation.

The Intelligence of Ordinary Things

Perhaps the most revealing objects in the exhibition are those that remain anonymous.

An African wooden strip-weaving loom and a carved wooden loom offer direct evidence of the technologies and ingenuity embedded in textile traditions. Their significance lies in the clarity with which they demonstrate the relationship between tool, material, and human action.

A vintage wooden distaff, used in preparing fibers for spinning, belongs to the same world of practical knowledge. Such objects illuminate the processes that precede the finished textile, processes that Hicks has spent a lifetime investigating and reinventing.

Other objects extend the conversation beyond weaving. A traditional Southeast Asian lidded box, fashioned from tree bark, rattan, and rope, reveals the possibilities of lightweight natural materials. A Japanese ranma tabletop screen displays the refinement of wooden construction, while a group of ceramic bowls and pinch pots celebrates the irregularities of hand-formed clay.

There are also objects whose appeal appears to reside in their eccentricity: two miniature metal irons, two tiny clay pots, handmade flutes, a stone figurative sculpture, and a pair of vintage snowshoes.

A pair of snowshoes, for example, might seem far removed from contemporary fiber art, yet their combination of wooden frames and interlaced fibers belongs to the same extended family of structural thinking as weaving. Similarly, the miniature irons suggest an appreciation for the tools and rituals associated with domestic labor.

The selection is refreshingly idiosyncratic. Rather than following a single geography, period, or aesthetic movement, it moves freely between cultures and categories. A Japanese cabinet sits within the same imaginative universe as a Mexican bird dish or a Southeast Asian bark container. Their original purposes differ, but each reveals a distinctive response to material, technique, and use.

What emerges is a portrait of Hicks as an observer of the designed world, attentive to objects that conventional histories of art and design might overlook.

Small Works, Expansive Ideas

Hicks’ own works provide another perspective on this dialogue with objects.

A large, untitled circular linen composition from 1977–78, measuring approximately 79 inches in diameter, demonstrates her interest in pushing textile techniques beyond conventional formats. Its scale and geometry invite consideration as both an artwork and an architectural presence.

Two wool prayer rugs from the 1970s similarly connect weaving to longstanding traditions of domestic and ceremonial textiles. Their presence alongside objects from different cultures reinforces the idea that textiles are never simply decorative surfaces. They can embody ritual, memory, labor, and social relationships.

The smaller works offer a more intimate encounter with Hicks’ imagination.

J. K. (Jacqueline Klugman), a woven silk-and-linen composition from 2008, and Loosely Speaking, made in Paris in 2006, belong to the artist’s continuing exploration of compact woven forms. After Andrea: On-the-Move (2012), inscribed “Agile Beyond Words,” suggests the mobility and wit that animate her approach to materials.

Even the titles reveal a playful sensibility. Do Not Disturb, a small French linen work dating to around 1980, and Let Loose (2018–19), composed of linen, cotton, and synthetic fibers, seem to propose opposing states of mind: restraint and abandon.

These works demonstrate that Hicks has never treated small scale as a limitation. A modest arrangement of fibers can embody the same intensity of observation and experimentation as a room-sized installation.

An Artist’s World, Revealed

What makes Favorite Odds and Ends particularly compelling is the opportunity to see Hicks’ work in the company of objects that rarely enter the same conversation as contemporary art.

For an artist whose career has challenged the marginalization of textiles within modern art, the exhibition offers a broader perspective on the cultural importance of everyday making.

A loom is not simply a tool. A basket is not merely a container. A staircase is more than a means of getting from one level to another. Each represents accumulated knowledge about materials, bodies, techniques, and the practical demands of living.

The exhibition also suggests a personal geography, one mapped not through countries or cities but through materials and forms. The objects bear witness to Hicks’ encounters with different traditions of making, although the circumstances of their acquisition remain largely outside the exhibition’s narrative.

There is something appealingly unguarded about this presentation. Rather than offering a tightly constructed account of artistic influences, Hicks allows the peculiarities of individual objects to come forward. The miniature irons, snowshoes, flutes, and ceramic vessels are not required to illustrate a grand theory. Their presence suggests pleasure, curiosity, and an enduring fascination with the ingenuity of others.

At the same time, the exhibition avoids treating Hicks’ own art as something isolated from the world around it. The Japanese staircases and Chinese barber stool are incorporated directly into new compositions, while the looms, baskets, and domestic implements illuminate the practical traditions that underpin her work.

In the end, Favorite Odds and Ends offers a reminder that artistic invention rarely occurs in isolation. It emerges through encounters with materials, techniques, places, and the work of other hands.

For Hicks, the extraordinary is often hiding in plain sight. All it takes is the willingness to look again.

Sheila Hicks | Favorite Odds and Ends is on view at Demisch Danant, New York, until December 19, 2026. The exhibition is part of the gallery’s Studio Visits | Creative Spaces and Collected Lives series.
